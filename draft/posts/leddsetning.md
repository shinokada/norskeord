The key is that a **subordinate clause (leddsetning)** is not defined by a particular word like *hvis* or *som*. It's defined by its **function in the sentence**.

## What is a subordinate clause?

A subordinate clause is a clause that **cannot normally stand alone as a complete sentence**. It depends on another clause.

### Main clause (helsetning)

Can stand alone:

> Han kommer.

> Det regner.

> Jeg er trøtt.

### Subordinate clause (leddsetning)

Depends on another clause:

> fordi han kommer

> at det regner

> hvis jeg er trøtt

These feel incomplete by themselves.

You normally need a main clause:

> Jeg går hjem **fordi han kommer**.

> Jeg vet **at det regner**.

> **Hvis jeg er trøtt**, legger jeg meg.

---

## The easiest test: "Can it stand alone?"

### Main clause

> Han kommer ikke.

✓ Complete sentence.

### Subordinate clause

> fordi han ikke kommer

✗ Incomplete.

You immediately want more information:

> Jeg er skuffet fordi han ikke kommer.

---

## The Norwegian word-order test

One of the best ways to recognize a subordinate clause is:

### Main clause

> Subject + Verb + Ikke

> Han **kommer ikke**.

> Det **er ikke** kaldt.

### Subordinate clause

> Subject + Ikke + Verb

> fordi han **ikke kommer**

> at det **ikke er** kaldt

This is why Norwegians often say:

> **Ikke før verbet = leddsetning.**

(Not always, but very often.)

---

## The three most common types

### 1. Content clauses (at-setninger)

They act like a noun.

> Jeg vet **at han ikke kommer**.

Question:

> What do I know?

Answer:

> at han ikke kommer

The whole clause is the object of *vet*.

---

### 2. Adverbial clauses

They give information about time, reason, condition, etc.

#### Reason

> Jeg ble hjemme fordi jeg ikke følte meg bra.

#### Condition

> Hvis det ikke regner, går vi ut.

#### Time

> Når hun ikke jobber, trener hun.

These answer questions like:

* Why?
* When?
* Under what condition?

---

### 3. Relative clauses (som-setninger)

They describe a noun.

> Mannen som ikke bor her.

Which man?

> som ikke bor her

The relative clause modifies *mannen*.

---

## Why "hvorfor" is tricky

Compare:

### Direct question (main clause)

> Hvorfor kommer han ikke?

This is a complete sentence.

Word order:

> hvorfor + kommer + han + ikke

### Embedded question (subordinate clause)

> Jeg vet ikke hvorfor han ikke kommer.

The clause:

> hvorfor han ikke kommer

cannot stand alone here.

Word order:

> hvorfor + han + ikke + kommer

---

## A practical checklist

When reading Norwegian, ask:

### 1. Is this a complete statement/question?

> Han kommer ikke.

→ Main clause.

### 2. Is it attached to another clause and giving extra information?

> fordi han ikke kommer

> hvis han ikke kommer

> som ikke kommer

> at han ikke kommer

→ Subordinate clause.

### 3. Does it have "ikke + verb" order?

> han ikke kommer

> det ikke er

That's a strong clue you're looking at a subordinate clause.

---

## The most common subordinate-clause starters

These often signal a subordinate clause:

| Type               | Words                                  |
| ------------------ | -------------------------------------- |
| Content            | at, om                                 |
| Condition          | hvis, dersom                           |
| Reason             | fordi, siden, ettersom                 |
| Time               | når, da, mens, før, etter at           |
| Contrast           | selv om, skjønt                        |
| Relative           | som                                    |
| Embedded questions | hvorfor, hva, hvem, hvor, hvordan, når |

Examples:

> Jeg vet **at han ikke kommer**.

> **Hvis det ikke regner**, går vi ut.

> Mannen **som ikke bor her**.

> Jeg vet ikke **hvorfor han ikke kommer**.

All of the bold parts are subordinate clauses.

A useful way to think about it is:

> A **main clause** can be a sentence by itself.
>
> A **subordinate clause** plays a role *inside* another sentence (as a reason, condition, description, object, question, etc.). That's why Norwegian usually moves **ikke** in front of the finite verb there.
